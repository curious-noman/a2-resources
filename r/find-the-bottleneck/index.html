BEFORE YOU START

What counts as a bottleneck?

A bottleneck is the part of a system that limits how quickly the whole process can produce a useful result. In service work, it can be a person, approval rule, queue, missing document, shared resource, or an external dependency.

Important distinction

The longest wait is a clue, not proof. A policy that only releases approvals on Fridays can create a long queue even when reviewers have enough total capacity. Likewise, one overloaded step may limit output even if its tasks are individually quick.

Don't optimize every activity. If drafting can handle 20 quotations a day, review handles 8, and sending handles 16, then review limits the end-to-end capacity to about 8 a day—provided the steps are sequential and the figures reflect realistic effective capacity.

Making drafting faster can raise unfinished work and waiting without increasing completed quotes. It does not automatically lower throughput; the problem is local optimization without improving overall flow.

FIELD STEP 01

Choose one process and define success.

A useful diagnosis starts with a clear boundary. Pick one workflow, not an entire department.

Write a process statement

When [trigger happens], we [steps] so that [customer outcome happens].

Example: “When a prospect requests pricing, we prepare, approve, and send an accurate quote so they can decide.”

STARTWhat exact event starts the clock? E.g. inquiry received.
ENDWhat customer-visible output ends it? E.g. approved quote sent.
UNIT OF WORKOne quote, one invoice, one onboarding case—not a vague task.
SUCCESS MEASUREEnd-to-end lead time, completed outputs per week, and accuracy.

Scope test: Can you follow a single case from start to finish and name who owns each handoff? If not, narrow the process further.

Start with 10–20 comparable cases if you have them; use a longer period when volume is low or work varies greatly. One formula worth knowing: Little's Law

For a reasonably stable, consistently measured workflow: average WIP = throughput rate × average lead time. Example: 24 unfinished quotes ÷ 8 completed quotes/day ≈ 3 working days average lead time. Do not treat this as a reliable forecast when the queue is continuously growing.

FIELD STEP 04

Locate the system constraint.

Look for where work waits persistently and where sustainable capacity fails to match the work arriving.

Use three checks together

  1. Queue trend: Is the waiting pile repeatedly growing rather than occasionally spiking?
  2. Capacity and demand: Can the step handle the average incoming workload, including variation and rework?
  3. Downstream evidence: Are later steps often waiting for this step, while earlier steps keep producing more?

Check age of the oldest item, priority changes, batching, shared staff, unavailable inputs, and rework. A visible queue can be a symptom of a different constraint, so confirm against actual case timestamps and conversations.

Decision rule: If demand exceeds the limiting step's sustainable capacity, capacity is a likely constraint. If all capacities exceed demand but waiting is still high, investigate batching, scheduling, prioritization, handoffs and variability.

FIELD STEP 05

Ask why the work is stuck.

Use 5 Whys to investigate the mechanism, then verify the answers using records and the people involved.

WHY 1Why are quotes sent late?They wait for approval.
WHY 2Why does approval have a queue?The manager completes about 8 reviews while 12 arrive daily.
WHY 3Why only 8?Reviews require repeated manual checks and context gathering.
WHY 4Why is context missing?The quote template does not capture standard risk and pricing criteria.
WHY 5Why not?Approval rules and templates haven't been reviewed as the workload grew.

This chain is a hypothesis for the teaching example, not a verified diagnosis. Real workflows can have several causes. Interview staff, inspect rejected quotes, and compare the policy with actual review history before changing controls.

FIELD STEP 06

Fix the constraint, not the symptom.

Theory of Constraints gives a practical order of operations. Don't jump straight to buying software.

01 / IDENTIFY

Locate the true limiting point

Use measured output, queues, and demand. In the example, approval is the candidate constraint.

02 / EXPLOIT

Get more from the existing constraint

Remove duplicate checks, standardize the required information, reserve review time, and prevent incomplete submissions from reaching the reviewer.

03 / SUBORDINATE

Align surrounding work

Don't push extra drafts into the same queue. Prioritize complete, ready-to-review requests and limit unnecessary WIP.

04 / ELEVATE

Increase capacity only if needed

Consider delegation within approved limits, a second qualified reviewer, tooling, or automation with appropriate human oversight.

05 / REPEAT

Find the next constraint

Once approval is no longer limiting, examine the next queue. The bottleneck may move to drafting, delivery, or even demand.

Where AI belongs

Only consider it after locating the cause. AI might help summarize documents or prepare complete review packets. It should not silently approve consequential exceptions. If a fixed rule works, a rule is usually easier to verify and maintain.

FIELD STEP 07

Prove the change and keep watching.

Use DMAIC thinking: measure the baseline, change one thing, compare fairly, control what works.

  1. Baseline: Record arrivals, completions, oldest waiting item, median/p90 lead time and rework rate for a representative period.
  2. Pilot: Choose one change, one owner, a clear start date and a defined group of similar cases.
  3. Compare: Did more correct work reach the customer? Did the queue shrink? Did rework or risk increase?
  4. Standardize: Update the process map, roles, rules, exception handling and monitoring.
  5. Repeat: Recheck the biggest queue because improvement may reveal a new constraint.
HYPOTHETICAL IMPROVEMENT

What changes if approvals reach 15 per day?

With 12 new requests per day and the same other-stage capacities (20 drafting, 16 sending), the system could now keep up with new demand. If an existing 20-quote backlog remains and approval truly has 3 spare slots/day, that backlog would take approximately 7 working days to clear, assuming the inputs are ready and conditions stay stable.

The goal isn't “reviewers are busier.” It's more accurate quotes delivered on time, with manageable WIP and no degradation in quality.
